<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Your NRR hits 115% and the board approves. Then two enterprise accounts pause expansion. Suddenly that number feels less solid. Strong net revenue retention looks like validation—until a few large accounts stop growing and the total hides what&#8217;s really happening.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This article explains what NRR means, how to calculate it, what good looks like by business model, and how to improve it.</span></p>
<h2>Main Takeaways</h2>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Net revenue retention (NRR) measures revenue growth from existing customers after accounting for expansion, contraction, and customer churn.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">NRR above 100% means your base grows without new logos. Below 100%, acquisition must offset losses.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">A wide gap between NRR and gross revenue retention (GRR) signals that expansion from a few accounts is masking broader churn.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">NRR benchmarks vary by business model. Enterprise SaaS often exceeds 120%. SMB products may sit below 100%.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Track NRR and GRR together to spot whether retention strength is spread across your base or concentrated in a few accounts.</span></li>
</ul>

<h2>What Is NRR?</h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Net revenue retention (NRR)—also called net dollar retention (</span><a href="https://www.gainsight.com/blog/preserving-revenue-with-px-how-measuring-ndr-is-easier-with-retention-focus/"><span style="font-weight: 400;">NDR</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">) or net retention rate—is the percentage of recurring revenue you keep from existing customers over a set period. Three forces shape it: expansion revenue from upsells, cross-sells, and seat additions; contraction from downgrades; and churn from cancellations.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In customer success, NRR connects CS team performance directly to revenue. It shows whether the customers your CS team manages are growing, staying flat, or shrinking in value—making it one of the most important numbers a CS leader owns.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">NRR matters most in SaaS and subscription businesses. It&#8217;s revenue-weighted rather than logo-based, so it tells you whether your existing dollars are growing—not just whether customers are staying. When NRR is above 100%, your base grows without a single new deal. Below 100%, you&#8217;re losing ground and acquisition must make up the difference.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">NRR is a lagging indicator. It confirms decisions customers already made—to expand, downgrade, or leave. By the time a weak NRR appears in your quarterly report, the retention problem is months old. That&#8217;s why NRR should always be tracked alongside leading indicators like health scores and product usage trends.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">NRR is also a strong proxy for customer lifetime value (LTV). When NRR is high, customers stay longer and spend more—which means they&#8217;re becoming more valuable over time, not just staying put. That&#8217;s what drives the valuation premium high-NRR companies command.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Unlike GRR, NRR includes expansion. That makes it a fuller picture of customer economics—but it can also hide problems. We cover that dynamic in the GRR vs. NRR section below.</span></p>
<h2>How to Calculate NRR: Formula and Example</h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The net revenue retention formula divides end-of-period</span><a href="https://www.gainsight.com/essential-guide/recurring-revenue/"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">recurring revenue</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> from existing customers by the starting figure.</span></p>
<p><b>NRR = (Starting MRR + Expansion MRR − Contraction MRR − Churned MRR) ÷ Starting MRR × 100</b></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>Starting MRR</b><span style="font-weight: 400;">: recurring revenue from existing customers at the start of the period</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>Expansion MRR</b><span style="font-weight: 400;">: revenue added from upsells, cross-sells, or seat additions</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>Contraction MRR</b><span style="font-weight: 400;">: revenue lost to downgrades</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>Churned MRR</b><span style="font-weight: 400;">: revenue lost from cancellations</span></li>
</ul>
<p><b>NRR calculation example:</b><span style="font-weight: 400;"> You start the month with $500,000 in monthly recurring revenue (MRR). Upsells add $75,000. Downgrades cost $20,000. Cancellations remove $30,000.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">($500,000 + $75,000 − $20,000 − $30,000) ÷ $500,000 × 100 = </span><b>105% NRR</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">You grew revenue from your existing base by 5%—before signing a single new deal.</span></p>
<p><b>Why composition matters as much as the number.</b><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Consider two companies that both end the month with $1.4M in total MRR.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Company A started with $1M, added $600K in new logos, gained $50K in expansion, and lost $250K to churn. NRR: </span><b>80%</b><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Growth is being driven by new acquisition—but the existing base is shrinking.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Company B started with $1M, added no new logos, gained $450K in expansion, and lost only $50K to churn. NRR: </span><b>140%</b><span style="font-weight: 400;">. The existing base is compounding on its own.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Same ending MRR. Completely different business health. Company A is filling a leaky bucket with new customers. Company B&#8217;s existing customers are becoming more valuable over time. NRR is the metric that reveals which story is true.</span></p>
<p><b>Period consistency matters.</b><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Every input must use the same time window. MRR-based calculation requires monthly expansion, contraction, and churn figures. Annual recurring revenue (ARR)-based calculation requires annual figures. Mixing periods produces wrong results.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">For usage-based pricing models, the same formula applies. Expansion and contraction reflect usage changes rather than discrete upgrade events. Snapshot revenue at consistent intervals and classify net usage changes into the right category.</span></p>
<h3>Why NRR Tells You What MRR and ARR Can&#8217;t</h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">MRR and ARR measure revenue from active customers, but neither shows what happens to that revenue over time. A company&#8217;s ARR can grow while NRR quietly falls. New logo acquisition temporarily offsets base erosion. NRR removes that blind spot by measuring net revenue change from existing customers directly. If NRR is falling while ARR is rising, the growth is masking a problem.</span></p>

<h2>What Is a Good Net Revenue Retention Rate? Benchmarks by Business Model</h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">No single number separates good from bad NRR. Your target depends on your annual contract value (ACV), customer segment, and current market. These ranges are a useful starting point:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>Below 90%</b><span style="font-weight: 400;">: Urgent. You&#8217;re losing significant revenue to churn or contraction. Find the root cause now.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>90–100%</b><span style="font-weight: 400;">: Your base is contracting. Growth depends entirely on new acquisition to offset losses.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>100–110%</b><span style="font-weight: 400;">: Solid. Existing customers are driving organic revenue growth.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>Above 120%</b><span style="font-weight: 400;">: Exceptional. Typical of high-growth enterprise SaaS with strong expansion motions.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Private B2B SaaS medians sit at roughly 101% NRR and 90% GRR, according to</span><a href="https://www.cfodesk.co.il/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/2024_kbcm_sapphire_saas_survey.pdf"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">KBCM + Sapphire Ventures</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Among public companies, the median has cooled to around 111%—its lowest point in years, per</span><a href="https://www.meritechcapital.com/blog/meritech-software-pulse-or-07-mar-2024"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">Meritech Capital</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">.</span></p>
<h3>SaaS Benchmarks by ACV and Business Model</h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">NRR expectations vary significantly by segment. Here&#8217;s what good looks like across business models:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>Enterprise SaaS (six-figure ACV):</b><span style="font-weight: 400;"> NRR above 120% is common. Expansion is built into how enterprise customers grow—more seats, more modules, more usage. Public companies like Zoom (~140%), PagerDuty (~139%), and Smartsheet (~135%) show what&#8217;s possible at the high end.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>Mid-market SaaS ($3M–$20M ARR):</b><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Median NRR sits at 104%, with top performers reaching 118%, according to</span><a href="https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/benchmarking-metrics-for-bootstrapped-saas-companies/"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">SaaS Capital</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>SMB-focused products:</b><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Account churn runs higher and per-account expansion is small. NRR below 100% isn&#8217;t automatically a crisis. For very low-price or B2C subscription products, GRR or a logo-based</span><a href="https://www.gainsight.com/blog/what-is-a-customer-retention-rate-and-how-do-i-calculate-it/"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">customer retention rate</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> may be more meaningful.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><b>NRR and valuation multiples.</b><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Investors pay close attention to NRR because it answers one question: do existing customers become more valuable over time? According to</span><a href="https://softwareequity.com/research/annual-saas-report"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">Software Equity Group</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, NRR above 110% is a meaningful driver of valuation upside for public SaaS companies. Companies that exceed this threshold see material multiple expansion, reflecting durable customer expansion and strong go-to-market execution.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Set your NRR target based on your ACV, customer mix, and market—not a one-size-fits-all benchmark.</span></p>
<h2>Who Owns NRR?</h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">NRR reflects company-wide effort—not just what the CS team does. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Customer Success has the most direct impact: CSMs drive adoption, manage renewal risk, and identify expansion opportunities. But other teams matter too:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>Sales</b><span style="font-weight: 400;"> sets customer expectations at close. Misaligned expectations lead to early churn that CS can&#8217;t fully recover.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>Product</b><span style="font-weight: 400;"> determines whether customers get enough value to stay and expand. Low feature adoption and slow time-to-value are product problems that show up in NRR.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><b>Marketing</b><span style="font-weight: 400;"> influences expansion through campaigns built around the patterns that correlate with high-NRR accounts.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The most effective way to establish shared accountability is to show each team how their specific work connects to NRR movement. When sales, product, and marketing all understand their role in the number, NRR stops being a CS metric and starts being a company metric.</span></p>
<h2>GRR vs. NRR: How They Work Together</h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Gross revenue retention (GRR) shows how much recurring revenue survives before any expansion enters the picture. It captures only contraction and customer churn. Because it strips out upsells and seat growth, GRR can never exceed 100%.</span></p>
<p><b>Gross revenue retention formula:</b></p>
<p><b>GRR = (Starting MRR − Contraction MRR − Churned MRR) ÷ Starting MRR × 100</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Using the same figures: $500,000 starting MRR, $20,000 contraction, $30,000 churn.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">($500,000 − $20,000 − $30,000) ÷ $500,000 × 100 = </span><b>90% GRR</b></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Compare that to 105% NRR from the same period. The entire 15-point gap comes from $75,000 in expansion revenue.</span></p>

<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">For CS teams, GRR is often the better day-to-day KPI. It gives a cleaner read on how much revenue is leaving due to churn and contraction—without expansion activity obscuring the signal.</span></p>
<h3>When the NRR-GRR Spread Signals Concentration Risk</h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A healthy NRR can hide a business that&#8217;s quietly eroding. Say a company has $1M in starting MRR. Five large accounts drive $300K in expansion while 50 smaller accounts churn out $200K. The result is 110% NRR—a strong-looking number. But GRR sits at 80%. That 30-point spread means the headline is being carried by a few large accounts while the broader base bleeds.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">When the NRR-GRR gap exceeds 30 points, you&#8217;re looking at concentration risk—not retention strength. If those expanding accounts slow down or leave, NRR collapses fast.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Ask yourself: what share of expansion comes from your top 10% of accounts? If the answer is more than half, you don&#8217;t have a retention success story. The expansion tailwind that propped up NRR for years is fading—seat growth across SaaS has stalled at just 2.2%, per </span><a href="https://research.etr.ai/etr-data-drop/saas-growth-slows-as-ai-spend-rises-signal-or-head-fake"><span style="font-weight: 400;">ETR data</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Track both metrics in every board report. A widening gap is a signal to investigate, not celebrate.</span></p>
<h2>How to Improve Net Revenue Retention</h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Tracking NRR is necessary. Knowing where to pull the levers is what moves it. Here are the highest-impact areas.</span></p>
<h3>1. Operationalize Your Customer Success Motion</h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Standardized playbooks, health score–triggered interventions, and automated lifecycle workflows make retention outcomes consistent. When CS runs as a repeatable system, NRR reflects the strength of the motion—not the strength of a few high performers.</span></p>
<h3>2. Segment Customers by Business Value</h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Not all churn hits equally. Losing a high-value account with strong expansion potential damages NRR far more than losing a low-spend account. Build retention programs that match the risk and opportunity each segment represents. Deliver product value fast for high-value segments. Use community, knowledge base, and product academy to support lower-touch segments without requiring proportional CSM time.</span></p>
<h3>3. Use Health Scores to Time Expansion Conversations</h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Expansion revenue is essential for NRR above 100%—but timing matters. Approaching too early signals that the CSM is prioritizing revenue over customer success. Too late, and the window closes. Health scores surface the behavioral signals that show a customer is ready to grow: license utilization thresholds, feature adoption milestones, and consistent engagement patterns.</span></p>
<h3>4. Create a Single Source of Truth for Customer Data</h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">NRR improvement requires cross-functional coordination. When CS, Sales, and Product all work from the same customer health, usage, and sentiment data, expansion conversations happen at the right time, churn signals reach the right team early, and product investment goes toward features that drive adoption.</span></p>
<h3>5. Learn From Churned Accounts</h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Churned customers are one of the most underused data sources in retention strategy. A simple post-churn survey surfaces the reasons behind revenue loss that health scores and usage data often miss—price sensitivity, missing features, poor onboarding, competitive displacement. Without asking, you&#8217;re optimizing based only on the customers who stayed.</span></p>
<h3>6. Align CS Compensation to Expansion Outcomes</h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">CS teams that carry only retention metrics have limited personal incentive to surface expansion opportunities proactively. Adding a variable pay component tied to expansion revenue—upsells, cross-sells, seat additions—helps direct CSM energy toward growth conversations, not just renewal protection. This doesn&#8217;t mean turning CSMs into salespeople. It means creating a structure where proactive expansion is recognized and rewarded—which reinforces the behaviors that move NRR above 100% and keeps it there.</span></p>
